# Guiding principles

The ideas every rule of the game should follow, the most important first.

1. **The land decides where producers come up.** Farms, orchards, fisheries, mines, quarries and
   mills appear more often where the land suits them, and stand for as long as the land is theirs.
2. **Producers decide where consumers can survive.** Homes, shops and works live where what they
   need is made within reach, and die out where it is not.
3. **The player is rewarded for making people and industry more satisfied by building efficient
   flows of goods.** Trains pay for bringing goods to where they are missing, filling the gaps in
   the markets, and what they bring makes the places they serve grow.

## How the game is made

The ideas every change to the game should follow.

- **The game is a simulation, not a script.** Towns, works and prices come out of simple rules of
  land, needs and deliveries. Results are never set by hand: when something is missing, the rule
  is fixed, not a building put in.
- **One rule, no exceptions.** Numbers follow from formulas, such as an amount from its worth in
  dollars or a level from merging two. An exception for one good or one building means the rule
  is wrong.
- **Everything is open and can be explained.** The player can always find out why something came
  up, stands still, costs what it does or does not grow. There are no hidden limits or secret
  multipliers, and every number sits at the building it belongs to.
- **The player earns from what they add.** Money comes from filling the gaps in the economy and
  from what would not exist without the player's railway, never from distance alone.
- **Hard but fair.** Costs keep the player under pressure; every choice gives something and takes
  something, and no option is pointless. Micromanagement pays, and technology takes it off the
  player over time.
- **Reality inspires, fun decides.** History, geography and technology are real where that makes
  the game interesting; prices and amounts reward doing hard things rather than copy the real
  world.
- **One world: a moving model railway.** One style, one scale measured by houses and one model at
  every zoom. The look tells what a thing is: a works' colours match what it makes and a
  building's size what it holds.
- **The game runs smoothly in the browser.** The web build is the main platform, and every new
  mechanic has to fit in it.

## The goal

- The player's aim is what their company is worth: its cash less any debt, its shares at what they
  would fetch today, and half of what its stations, track and trains would cost today.
- Each difficulty sets one target for 1 January 2050; the game says when it is reached, and on
  that day whether it was.
- Debt is allowed but not for long: six months in a row begun with the cash below nothing bankrupt
  the company. The player is warned the moment the cash goes below nothing and sees the months
  left.
- The game never ends on its own: the target for 1 January 2050 is a measure, not an ending, and
  play goes on after it. Only going bankrupt ends a game.

## The rules that follow from them

- The player earns only when buildings take goods out of the player's stations. Goods handed out
  on their own earn nothing, so the money comes from what the player adds to the local economy:
  a station is an investment.
- Value comes from scarcity, not distance. Everything a station hands out earns its freight, a
  fifth of the good's market price, what trains brought and what the station gathered alike, up to
  three times that the less satisfied the building that took it was over the last quarter.
  Passengers and mail pay their freight times the share of the station's demand left.
- Buildings hand out what they make to the buildings around them on their own, everywhere on the
  map, the nearest first, up to six cells away. Nothing is lost on the way, but the further a buyer
  is, the less of the maker's month it can take, by the Fibonacci numbers: all of it in the same
  cell, then 13, 8, 5, 3, 2 and 1 twenty-firsts of it from one to six cells away.
- Only what is left after that goes to a station within reach, the nearest one first, stations as
  near sharing it by the room they have left; stations never take a good the buildings around
  could have used.
- A station hands out all it holds, what trains brought and what it gathered itself, the same
  way buildings hand out their own goods: the player's own buildings first, then the nearest
  first, buildings as near sharing by how short they are, but without the falloff within its
  delivery reach. Where a starving building stands decides whether it is fed.
- A station not joined by track to another station gathers and hands out nothing.
- The player can buy shares of buildings, a thousand to a building, traded 100, 200 or 600 at a
  time. With 60% of a building's shares the player controls it: their flag flies big over it and
  only then do limestone and machinery work on it, on all of it; more
  shares only raise their part. A whole
  building costs ten years of all it makes at full satisfaction, two thirds of that at none
  and twice at 200%, by how satisfied it was over the last quarter, and shares bought can be sold
  only five years later. The player's part of what it makes goes into the stations around it
  first, before anything is handed out, and the stations buy it from the player at the market
  price as it goes in.
- The demand at a station counts only what the buildings around it were left short of after the
  goods handed out on their own.
- Buildings come up at their base chance anywhere the land allows, and breed by how satisfied they
  are, the more the more of what draws them stands around.
- Producers of what the land gives never starve to death; fields go only when a town grows over
  them. Homes starving for ten years die out, everything else after thirty.
- A processor is satisfied only by what it makes and sells and by the bonus goods it builds
  with, such as bricks and cement for factories, never by a full stockpile.

## Economy

- Margins shrink as the world fills up: supply grows far faster than demand, so a unit earns less
  and less, and to stay profitable the player has to carry more for less, investing well enough to
  keep buying the newest trains.
- Supply and demand grow by tiles: every good belongs to a tile on two boards, supply and demand.
  Every year two supply tiles and one demand tile go up a level, the ones the player picks, the
  lowest for picks not made, and no tile gets more than two levels above the lowest on its board.
- As a year turns the game waits for the picks: production cards first, then consumption.
  Each level adds a flat share of the tile's base, not compounded, by good and period.
- Every supply level also puts up buildings that make the tile's goods, each where it is likeliest
  to come up: what the land gives several at a time, works one or two at a time in turn.
- Supply raises only what a building makes and demand only what it takes, never both: more supply
  makes more from the same inputs, more demand takes more for as much.
- Eras come when every supply tile reaches a level, each with a generation of wagons and new tiles,
  which start at the lowest level on each board. Only coal closes, around 2000.
- Prices follow a price list, not a market: a tile's demand over its supply decides what a unit of
  its goods earns, it changes only when a tile goes up, and the player sees what a pick does to it
  before making it.
- At the start everything pays and everything is short, food most of all; growing towns make
  demand, and someone to sell large amounts to.
- Most money comes from the needs of grown homes, not from food or passengers.
- Goods fall into categories of need: food, processed food, common goods and heating. Any kind of
  good in a category meets that need.
- Base goods are needed; comforts and novelties, such as newspapers, chairs or beer, are only a
  bonus. A house takes common goods only as a bonus; tenements and flats must have them.
- What buildings must have is handed out before any bonus, both by buildings on their own and by
  stations, so nearby homes don't thrive on extras while those further off go short. Homes want
  twice the food they must eat; the rest is a bonus.
- Goods are handed out from the most valuable to the cheapest: meat before wheat.
- Local needs come first: a station does not take away a good the place it was made is short of.
- Satisfaction counts what actually arrived; bonus goods can lift it above 100%.
- Buyers are hard to satisfy in full: demand is bigger than supply, so bringing more always pays.
- A train can always unload; goods are used up the moment they arrive.
- Only what went over the player's track earns money.
- Prices are not meant to be realistic: they reward doing something hard. Hauling food and raw
  goods pays little, about enough for the stations and track; passengers and processed goods pay
  the most, since most of their demand and people exist because of the player's network. Every
  further stage of a chain pays more. What a building earns a month at full output matters more
  than the price of a unit.
- Every car costs something each time it leaves a stop, loaded or not, more the bigger it is, so
  short, well-managed trains pay.
- A home that grows wants more of everything: its reward for growing is that it uses more.
- Blocks of flats are the reward: they take a lot of the late goods, such as cars, appliances and
  cola; tenements take the same things, but less for each person living there.
- Demand tiles open new buyers as they go up, so a level brings something more than its share:
  cola for every home, cars and appliances first for tenements and a level later for houses,
  leather for furniture makers' upholstery and later for car seats.
- A bonus input raises output but never replaces the main input.
- Raw goods are worked before buildings use them: logging camps fell logs, and only a sawmill's
  planks and wood chips go to other buildings. Homes and wood-burning engines burn logs as they are.
- A boost of a works either makes more from the same inputs or lets it take in more of them, as
  machinery and diesel let a sawmill saw more logs, still a plank from every log.
- Hard chains, with many inputs, make few but valuable goods.
- Works grow by merging: three food makers of a kind become a big one and three big ones a mega
  one; the harder processors merge two at a time.
- Towns start from simple local food makers, such as bakeries, not from farms.
- Venues, such as taverns and clubs, make the homes around them grow faster.
- Builder's yards, fed with bricks, cement and glass, make the homes and works around them come up
  faster.
- Every place that sends passengers belongs to a town. Whenever one is built, it and every such place
  next to it take the colour sending the most passengers there, and any other town with a place
  there joins that colour whole, so towns that grow into each other merge and never touch. A river
  up to two cells wide doesn't split a town: the places on its two banks count as next to each
  other. Stations belong to the town around them, checked every year, and passengers and mail only
  travel between stations of different towns.
- What trains pay to burn coal, wood or diesel is priced apart from what hauling that good pays.
- Every fuel price is a tug of war: demand, the player's own included, against supply, measured
  against the map's starting state so every map is fair.
- Fuel bought out of the player's own stations is cheaper than at retail, and technology narrows
  the gap.
- Infrastructure the player owns, such as power plants, has to pay off by making a lot.
- The world moves through history: dated events (wars, crises, inventions) change what is
  wanted, made and paid. Each one is news the player is told of, with what it changes, so nothing
  shifts unannounced.
- History is the same in every game, apart from what happens only in one part of the world, such
  as the market opening in the old Eastern Bloc in 1989.
- Where an amount is set by worth, it is worked out from the good's price, so a new price rescales
  it on its own: a port's monthly amount is its tier's worth in dollars over the good's price.
- Passengers and mail wait only a month: every month brings new ones and the old ones are gone,
  which is what makes expresses pay.

## Trains and technology

- The game is hard: its costs keep the player under pressure.
- Technology is earned only by delivering goods of its category, more for every point of the
  buildings of that tree the player owns.
- Technology takes micromanagement off the player over time, with general cars and bigger cars, so
  they can move on to the next needs.
- Physical limits shape the network: a train's fuel range makes the player build refuelling stops.
- Each generation and kind of engine is a trade-off with strengths and weaknesses; no option is
  pointless.
- New technology comes in big price steps, three times the price each generation.
- Fuel prices rise by themselves as demand for fuel grows; only station upkeep rises by a fixed 2%
  a year.
- Bigger things cost disproportionately more to keep: a station's upkeep doubles with each level
  and rises every year.
- The player can see why numbers changed: charts and breakdowns of prices and spending.
- Track is a network, not links from A to B. A train always takes the shortest way through it and
  may turn round at a station.
- A train pulls several cars, not one: car sizes are chosen so a usual run fills several.
- Trains do not pass through each other: one stops and lets the other by. Every train speeds up
  gradually, a better one faster.
- Changing a train's cargo or route does not break off its run; the change applies from its next
  visit.
- What a train's cars and goods weigh slows it and makes it burn more, but never shortens its
  range, which would confuse the player.
- How fast a train picks up speed depends on its engine, not on its cars.
- An engine's pull is a fixed number of tonnes of its own and never grows with the cars: a
  freight engine pulls just a full train of coal in the cars of the era it comes in, so with the
  bigger cars of a later era it no longer manages a full train.

## How the map grows

- The map grows by itself; the player changes where it grows by delivering goods. Towns the player
  connects grow faster.
- One set of rules for the whole history: the years before play starts run on the same rules as
  play, with no special factors for them.
- A game starts on a honeycomb of seven hexagonal lots in the middle of its place, a quarter of its
  land. Every hex grows every month, so the time to set up a game and to run a month grows with
  the map; a quarter of the land starts and plays about three times as fast as the whole.
- More land is bought a lot at a time, each a seventh of the starting land, at Fibonacci prices
  that keep a big map hard to get. What lies past the map's edge shows before it is bought, so
  nothing in it is a surprise.
- Land bought grows on its own from the start of history to the year it is bought, under the
  same rules, and arrives as built up as the rest. It gets only a quarter of the buildings every
  map is sure to have, and the game's novelties come up there at most at a quarter of the chance
  they had over the years played, from the same pool.
- Every map gets at least a minimum of each key building. They come up on their own first; if too
  few do, the game adds the missing ones in a random but natural place for them.
- Every building that is not unique comes up at least once in the year it opens, once the map
  makes its main inputs, and then has odds high enough to grow on its own from there.
- Only the land limits where things are made: raw producers grow where the map suits them (rice
  within 41° of the equator, grapes where it is warm), and a works or venue opens only once
  something on the map makes each of its main inputs. No vineyard, no winery.
- Unique buildings come up in some games and not in others, two or three a game. They fit their era
  and only make or use goods the game already has.
- No hidden caps: every limit on where or how many buildings come up is a rule the player can
  read in the game, with the reason a chance is zero.

## Building

- Track goes round buildings: never under them. A building in the way of track, a station or a
  facility moves aside to a cell nearby, at a high price that rises with people's anger at the
  company. Bulldozing a building angers people the same way; the player's own buildings and
  track do not.
- Anything can be demolished, at a price: a house costs little, a factory a lot, and unique
  buildings cannot be removed.

## Look

- Hexagons, not squares.
- Track runs in eight directions along four axes: across, along both diagonals, and straight up and
  down. On a hex it leaves through the middle of one of the six edges into the neighbour, or
  through the top or bottom corner, running along the edge between the two cells beside it into
  the cell beyond. A piece of track turns at most 60°. A station's track runs straight along one
  of the four axes. A station is built turned towards track that points into its cell, and while
  no track joins it, the first track laid to it turns it to come in straight, from its side too.
  Once track joins it, its way never changes and other track joins it only at its two ends: track
  laid against its side runs on round into one of them.
- Track is dark and distinct, the same width whichever way it runs, and readable at every zoom. On
  hills and mountains it rises with the ground on a smoothed bed, with no spikes at bends.
- Of what is underground, only the entrances are drawn: tunnel portals.
- The perspective is consistent, as in Railroad Tycoon 2: track lies under the trees and stations
  stand turned along their track. The art is our own.
- A building stands on the land it works with: a logging camp among trees.
- Buildings fill a cell's six sixths in a ring, each sixth facing one edge of the hex.
- Everything that does not grow from the land (homes, works, shops, churches, ports) gets a cobbled
  road along the edges of the sixths it stands on; such cells side by side share a road along
  their common edge, and a loose piece of road joins the rest by the shortest way round the cells.
  A building standing alone, with no other such building in its cell or the cells beside it, gets
  a small cobbled square round it instead of roads. Fields, farms, mines, quarries and logging camps keep their own ground and no road. Track and
  stations never lay a road.
- Every kind of building is recognisable at a glance from a middle zoom and never looks like a
  house or like another kind: it shows what it is by its shape and by one subtle sign, such as a
  gilt diamond on a jeweller's roof, never by a cottage with a sign on it.
- Every level of a building looks different, not only bigger: big and mega works have their own
  look, the same trade grown grander, and a mega one is spectacular.
- The game looks like a moving model railway, not pixel art; every new piece of art serves that
  look.
- One model at every zoom: it is drawn once at its largest and shrunk, never drawn again for each
  zoom, so the style never drifts.
- At every zoom above 3 the ground looks the same.
- One scale for every building, measured by houses: a barn a touch bigger than a house, a works
  bigger than a tenement, a station fitting the buildings around it. Only what could not be read
  at its true size is drawn bigger: farm animals are twice their size.
- A building's colours follow what it makes: amber and copper for beer, white and blue for milk,
  glowing metal and steel grey for steel.
- Every building stands on the ground and inside its cell: nothing floats or sticks out of its hex.
- Fields and farms blend into the colour of the land under them.
- Trains, trees and buildings are realistic, like 3D models on a layout, never toys, with as much
  detail as can be drawn.
- A station looks its level and matches the other buildings.
- Rarity has the same colours everywhere: green common, blue rare, purple epic, gold legendary.
- Art is judged by eye first: a change to the art alone is shown in the game and the tests run
  only once its look is approved.

## Windows

- Every window always shows its title and an X to close it at its top. Both stay put: what the
  window holds scrolls under them and nothing covers them.
- A window's main button, such as Buy, is always in view and never scrolls away.
- Buying opens its own screen with the stats and a big picture of each choice, and what was bought
  is set up on that same screen.
- Something newly bought does not start until the player has finished setting it up.
- The default is the safer, less blocking choice: waiting for a full load is off.
- Choosing a cargo shows only what the station has, without scrolling, and what the next stop
  needs.
- A window shows no numbers that tell the player nothing at that moment.
- A pause always shows, readably, at the top in the middle of the screen.
- The game says why something stands still: a train waiting for a way says so.
