# Guiding principles

The ideas every rule of the game should follow.

1. **The land decides where producers come up.** Farms, orchards, fisheries, mines, quarries and
   mills appear more often where the land suits them, and stand for as long as the land is theirs.
2. **Producers decide where consumers can survive.** Homes, shops and works live where what they
   need is made within reach, and die out where it is not.
3. **The player is rewarded for making people and industry more satisfied by building efficient
   flows of goods.** Trains pay for bringing goods to where they are missing, filling the gaps in
   the markets, and what they bring makes the places they serve grow.

## The rules that follow from them

- Buildings hand out what they make to the buildings around them on their own, everywhere on the
  map, the nearest first, up to six cells away. Nothing is lost on the way, but the further a buyer
  is, the less of the maker's month it can take, by the Fibonacci numbers: all of it in the same
  cell, then 13, 8, 5, 3, 2 and 1 twenty-firsts of it from one to six cells away.
- Only what is left after that goes to a station within reach; stations never take a good the
  buildings around could have used.
- A station hands out what trains bring the same way buildings hand out their own goods: the
  nearest first, buildings as near sharing by how short they are, but without the falloff
  within its delivery reach. Where a starving building stands decides whether it is fed.
- The player earns only when buildings take goods out of the player's stations. Goods handed out
  on their own earn nothing, so the money comes from what the player adds to the local economy:
  a station is an investment. The player can buy shares of buildings, a thousand to a building,
  priced by how satisfied the building was over the last quarter. Their part of what it makes goes
  into the stations around it first, before anything is handed out, sold to the player at the
  market price as it goes in. Trains earn freight, a fifth of the market price, for what they
  bring that buildings take, more the less satisfied the building that took it was.
- The demand at a station counts only what the buildings around it were left short of after the
  goods handed out on their own.
- Buildings come up at their base chance anywhere the land allows, and breed by how satisfied they
  are, the more the more of what draws them stands around.
- Producers of what the land gives never starve to death; fields go only when a town grows over
  them. Homes starving for ten years die out, everything else after thirty.
- A processor is satisfied only by what it makes and sells, never by a full stockpile.
- Value comes from scarcity, not distance.

## How the map grows

- The map grows by itself; the player changes where it grows by delivering goods. Towns the player
  connects grow faster.
- One set of rules for the whole history: the years before play starts run on the same rules as
  play, with no special factors for them.
- A game starts on the square in the middle of its place, a quarter of its land. Every hex grows
  every month, so the time to set up a game and to run a month grows with the map; a quarter of
  the land starts and plays about three times as fast as the whole.
- More land is bought a lot at a time, each a quarter of the starting land, at Fibonacci prices
  that keep a big map hard to get. What lies past the map's edge shows before it is bought, so
  nothing in it is a surprise.
- Land bought grows on its own from the start of history to the year it is bought, under the
  same rules, and arrives as built up as the rest. It gets only a quarter of the buildings every
  map is sure to have, and the game's novelties come up there at most at a quarter of the chance
  they had over the years played, from the same pool.
- Every map gets at least a minimum of each key building. They come up on their own first; if too
  few do, the game adds the missing ones in a random but natural place for them.
- Every building that is not unique comes up at least once in the year it opens, and then has odds
  high enough to grow on its own from there.
- Unique buildings come up in some games and not in others, two or three a game. They fit their era
  and only make or use goods the game already has.

## Economy

- Most money comes from the needs of grown homes, not from food or passengers.
- Every place that sends passengers belongs to a town. Whenever one is built, it and every such place
  next to it take the colour sending the most passengers there, and any other town with a place
  there joins that colour whole, so towns that grow into each other merge and never touch. Stations belong to the town around them, checked every year, and passengers and mail
  only travel between stations of different towns.
- A home that grows wants more of everything: its reward for growing is that it uses more.
- Towns start from simple local food makers, such as bakeries, not from farms.
- Goods fall into categories of need: food, processed food, common goods and heating. Any kind of
  good in a category meets that need.
- Base goods are needed; comforts and novelties, such as newspapers, chairs or beer, are only a
  bonus.
- A bonus input raises output but never replaces the main input.
- Hard chains, with many inputs, make few but valuable goods.
- Works grow by merging: three food makers of a kind become a big one and three big ones a mega
  one; the harder processors merge two at a time.
- Venues, such as taverns and clubs, make the homes around them grow faster.
- A train can always unload; goods are used up the moment they arrive.
- Buyers are hard to satisfy in full: demand is bigger than supply, so bringing more always pays.
- Satisfaction counts what actually arrived; bonus goods can lift it above 100%.
- Local needs come first: a station does not take away a good the place it was made is short of.
- Goods are handed out from the most valuable to the cheapest: meat before wheat.
- Only what went over the player's track earns money. A station earns nothing unless track joins
  it to another station.
- Prices are not meant to be realistic: they reward doing something hard. Hauling food and raw
  goods pays little, about enough for the stations and track; passengers and processed goods pay
  the most, since most of their demand and people exist because of the player's network. Every
  further stage of a chain pays more. What a building earns a month at full output matters more
  than the price of a unit.
- What trains pay to burn coal, wood or diesel is priced apart from what hauling that good pays.
- Every car costs something each time it leaves a stop, loaded or not, more the bigger it is, so
  short, well-managed trains pay.
- Every price is a tug of war: demand, the player's own included, against supply, measured against
  the map's starting state so every map is fair.
- Fuel bought out of the player's own stations is cheaper than at retail, and technology narrows
  the gap.
- Infrastructure the player owns, such as power plants, has to pay off by making a lot.

## Trains and technology

- The game is hard: its costs keep the player under pressure.
- Fuel prices rise by themselves as demand for fuel grows; only station upkeep rises by a fixed 2%
  a year.
- Each generation and kind of engine is a trade-off with strengths and weaknesses; no option is
  pointless.
- New technology comes in big price steps, three times the price each generation.
- Technology takes micromanagement off the player over time, with general cars and bigger cars, so
  they can move on to the next needs.
- Technology is earned by delivering goods of its category.
- Physical limits shape the network: a train's fuel range makes the player build refuelling stops.
- Bigger things cost disproportionately more to keep: a station's upkeep doubles with each level
  and rises every year.
- The player can see why numbers changed: charts and breakdowns of prices and spending.
- The game never ends.
- Track is a network, not links from A to B. A train always takes the shortest way through it and
  may turn round at a station.
- A train pulls several cars, not one: car sizes are chosen so a usual run fills several.
- Trains do not pass through each other: one stops and lets the other by. Every train speeds up
  gradually, a better one faster.
- Changing a train's cargo or route does not break off its run; the change applies from its next
  visit.

## Building

- Track goes round buildings: never under them, and it never moves them.
- Anything can be demolished, at a price: a house costs little, a factory a lot, and unique
  buildings cannot be removed.

## Look

- Hexagons, not squares.
- Track runs in eight directions along four axes: across, along both diagonals, and straight up and
  down. On a hex it leaves through the middle of one of the six edges into the neighbour, or
  through the top or bottom corner, running along the edge between the two cells beside it into
  the cell beyond. A piece of track turns at most 60°. A station's track runs straight along one
  of the four axes. A station is built turned towards track that points into its cell, and while
  no track joins it, the first track laid to it turns it to come in straight, from its side too.
  Once track joins it, its way never changes and other track joins it only at its two ends: track
  laid against its side runs on round into one of them.
- Track is dark and distinct, the same width whichever way it runs, and readable at every zoom. On
  hills and mountains it rises with the ground on a smoothed bed, with no spikes at bends.
- Of what is underground, only the entrances are drawn: tunnel portals.
- The perspective is consistent, as in Railroad Tycoon 2: track lies under the trees and stations
  stand turned along their track. The art is our own.
- A building stands on the land it works with: a lumber mill among trees.
- Every kind of building is recognisable at a glance and never looks like a house or like another
  kind.
- Big and mega works have their own look, the same trade grown grander; a mega one is spectacular.

## Windows

- A window's main button, such as Buy, is always in view and never scrolls away.
- Buying opens its own screen with the stats and a big picture of each choice, and what was bought
  is set up on that same screen.
- Something newly bought does not start until the player has finished setting it up.
- The default is the safer, less blocking choice: waiting for a full load is off.
- Choosing a cargo shows only what the station has, without scrolling, and what the next stop
  needs.
- A window shows no numbers that tell the player nothing at that moment.
